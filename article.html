FTTx Network Deployment: Bridging the Last Mile

By Annie Thomas | Published 2026

In the modern telecommunications landscape, the need for high-speed, reliable internet has never been greater. As an Electronics and Computer Engineering student with hands-on experience in fiber optic communication, I have seen firsthand how Fiber to the x (FTTx) networks are transforming connectivity. FTTx is not just a technology; it is the backbone of modern digital infrastructure, replacing traditional copper networks to deliver unparalleled bandwidth and low latency.

The Core Components of FTTx

An FTTx deployment depends on different hardware materials to satisfy its functionality. The first material is the Optical Line Terminal (OLT) which acts as the starting point of signal transmission. Then the cables are run from there through the distribution network which has splitters to divide a signal to multiple customer premises. At the customer premises those cables are connected to the Optical Network Unit (ONU) or Optical Network Terminal (ONT) which converts light signal back to electrical signals. The conversion is important because it is the language user devices like routers and computers can understand. Understanding how these components connect is very crucial especially to any network technician.

The Deployment Process

During my industrial attachments at ESCOM OFC (Optical Fibre Communication), I learned that FTTx deployment is a procedural process. It begins with site survey and route and network planning, where engineers figure out the most efficient routes both in the systems and on site after network request. After finding the best possible route, the optical fibre cables are run from the nearest FAT to the customer premises. These cables are connected in the process called splicing which is done both at the FAT and customer premises when connecting them to the Optical Network Unit (ONU) or Optical Network Terminal (ONT). The whole process is done carefully to prevent micro-bends that can degrade the signal.

The optical fibre splicing process is the most critical process as it aims at reducing signal loss. It requires materials such as cleavers, strippers, cleaning wipes, sleeves, cable cutters, cable ties, and splicing machines. Before the process itself I had to know the colour code for optical fibre cores. Then using the fusion splicer, I aligned the fibre cores which I stripped and cleaned and fused them together using an electric arc. The process requires careful attention and concentration especially when testing individual cores before fusing them together. If there is more loss, I had to cut the core and strip it again, clean it, and then fuse it again until the loss is in the acceptable range. While one team is physically installing the cables, the NOC team was responsible for adding the customers into the system through network configurations. The systems are used to monitor network performance and usage and power in different areas in Malawi once physical installation was done, the internet access was verified at the customer site until it was successful. It was really a good experience for me and my friends there.

Challenges and Solutions

Network deployment comes with several challenges. Below are some of the key issues we faced and the solutions we implemented:

  • Physical Network Damage: Mostly occurred due to fibre cuts where the fibre cable was cut intentionally or by accidents. For example, we had a major fibre cut in Liwonde that disrupted services for major customers.
    Solution: We re-spliced cut and broken cores inside the junction boxes and cut fibres effectively until the network was restored.
  • Broken Cores: Occurred due to poor splicing. We found broken cores inside the junction box in Zomba as well as in Blantyre, which caused network outages in both places.
    Solution: We re-spliced the broken cores inside the junction boxes and effectively cut fibres until the network was restored.
  • Customer Absence: This happens when customers do not notify the team that they will not be available. This delayed our installation process as we had to return again for installation.
    Solution: We practiced effective communication by calling customers before going to their premises to confirm if they would be available.
  • Risk of Fiber Damage from Electricity Teams: Electricity teams sometimes replace poles, which puts our fiber cables at risk.
    Solution: In case of an outage ordered by the electricity team, we went there to repair our cables as soon as possible.

Lessons Learned

  • I gained practical skills in fibre splicing, climbing poles, raising cables to safer positions, and network configuration (VPN and FTTx).
  • Teamwork is crucial during field work as it helps to prevent accidents.
  • Being humble and open to learning helped me to gain a lot of practical knowledge.

Looking to the Future

FTTx technology is continually evolving, with GPON and XGS-PON pushing the boundaries of what is possible. As I approach graduation, I am eager to further develop my expertise in network infrastructure. The future of engineering is undeniably connected, and contributing to the deployment and maintenance of these critical networks is a challenge I am ready to embrace.
